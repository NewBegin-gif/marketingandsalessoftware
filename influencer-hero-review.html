Pricing (2026)

✓ Pricing re-verified 24 Sep 2026

(pricing matrix read 18 September 2026 in documentation the vendor sent us; the public ladder at influencer-hero.com/pricing was read the same week and shows the quarterly column) Four plans, and the figure you see depends entirely on the billing term you pick. Billed monthly: Standard $649, Pro $1,049, Business $2,490. Billed quarterly: $519, $839 and $1,992 — and this is the column the public pricing page leads with, which is why $519 is the number most people quote.

Billed annually: $454, $734 and $1,743. The advertised 20% and 30% savings check out exactly against the monthly rate ($649 × 0.8 = $519.20, × 0.7 = $454.30), so the discounts are real and the headline is simply the discounted one. What each plan carries: Standard is one seat, 1,000 reach-outs a month and 20 influencer payouts. Pro is three seats, 5,000 reach-outs and 200 payouts.

Business is eight seats, 10,000 reach-outs and 500 payouts, and Enterprise has unlimited payouts. On 19 September the Business plan card still said unlimited while the comparison grid on the same page said 500; Influencer Hero told us the card was a leftover from a site revamp, and when we re-read the page on 24 September 2026 the card and the grid both said 500 for Business and unlimited for Enterprise.

Enterprise is custom on every other axis. Every plan carries a three-month minimum term and there is no free trial, so the smallest real commitment is three months of Standard: $1,947 billed monthly, or $1,557 if you take the quarterly rate. The influencer CRM, the outreach automation and the UGC tracking are in every plan; the ladder is gated on quota and seats.

Influencer payouts can run through bank direct debit (ACH in the US, Bacs in the UK, SEPA in the EU) with only Stripe's processing fee passed through: 0.1% to 0.4% by the vendor's own account, around 0.4% to 0.5% effective by its Payout Manager post, with US ACH capped at $5 a payout, against the 2-3% that card processors typically charge. Plans change — always verify the live price on their site.

What the pricing page doesn’t say

The catch

Every plan carries it, so the smallest honest entry price is not $519 but three months — $1,557 on the quarterly rate, $1,947 if you pay monthly. An influencer programme takes longer than four weeks to show you anything, so the vendor is not being unreasonable; it just means the decision is a quarter of spending rather than a month of trying. Read which column you are quoted. The public pricing page leads with the quarterly rate, so $519 is what gets repeated — but billed monthly the same plan is $649, a quarter more.

Pro is $839 or $1,049, Business $1,992 or $2,490. The 20% and 30% savings are genuine and check out exactly against the monthly rate; the trap is only that the advertised number already has the discount in it. What the ladder actually meters is worth understanding, because it is better than it looks. A reach-out counts once, at the moment you first contact a new influencer. Every follow-up, reply and ongoing conversation with that same creator after that is unlimited and never draws the quota down again — so the meter counts new relationships opened, not messages sent.

Standard's 1,000 a month is therefore 1,000 new creators, which is a great many, and nurturing the ones who answer costs nothing. Hitting the cap gives you two routes and the vendor recommends one of them against its own short-term interest: buy a top-up, or move up a tier. Their own documentation says brands who top up once tend to keep doing it, so repeated top-ups erode the saving from staying low.

No top-up rate is published anywhere, which is the one number still missing from an otherwise unusually open account. Payouts are capped per plan, and the cap is what pushes paying brands up the ladder: 20 a month on Standard, 200 on Pro, 500 on Business and unlimited only on Enterprise. The Business card briefly said unlimited after a site revamp; the vendor corrected it, and on 24 September 2026 the pricing page gave 500 on both the card and the comparison grid.

Seats follow the quota — one, three, eight — so a two-person team is pushed to Pro by headcount alone, whatever its volume.

What Influencer Hero does

All-in-one influencer marketing platform: AI creator discovery, automated outreach, an influencer CRM, UGC tracking and affiliate management on one record per creator, with 45-plus native integrations and payouts run through bank debit at 0.1-0.4% rather than 2-3% card fees. Every plan carries the full toolset — the ladder is gated on quota and seats. A reach-out is counted once when a new influencer is first contacted — all later messaging with that creator is unlimited. Three-month minimum on every plan and no free trial.

What buyers report about Influencer Hero

Recurring themes from the buyer-review sites listed at the foot of this page, read in September 2026. We have not run Influencer Hero ourselves, so this is other buyers' experience rather than ours. We summarise it here because the complaints are usually the part a vendor page leaves out.

What they praise
  • • The AI that reads the email thread with an influencer and fills in their shipping details, plus creating orders and affiliate discounts inside the app so they sync straight to Shopify — a Canadian store two months in names this as the thing that removed the manual back-and-forth (April 2026).
  • • Outreach and gifting at scale. A US brand running two brands through one account rates the mass email and centralised onboarding above working through DMs or TikTok Shop affiliates (August 2025).
  • • Reporting per creator. Reviewers single out being able to pull click and engagement data on any influencer rather than judging by follower count.
  • • Named account managers. Several reviews credit a specific person for onboarding and ongoing help, which fits a product that no longer lets you sign yourself up.
What they complain about
  • • A payout fee nobody mentioned. A UK merchant paying over $5,000 of affiliate commission reports a 1.5% charge for processing the payment, on top of the 10% they owed the influencers and the cost of shipping product (May 2024). That 1.5% reflects the old setup: Influencer Hero told us in September 2026 that payouts have since been rebuilt so brands can fund them by bank direct debit (ACH, Bacs or SEPA) with only Stripe's fee passed through, which it puts at 0.1% to 0.4%; its Payout Manager post (influencer-hero.com/blogs/payout-manager) gives around 0.4% to 0.5% effective and caps US ACH at $5 a payout. Card payouts still cost the standard Stripe rate, so pick the method before you budget.
  • • Built for a bigger organisation than it looks. A US small business with nobody dedicated to the tool describes the self-directed platform as an agency and mid-to-large-brand product and recommends the fully managed option instead (edited June 2025).
  • • Import and export. An Irish brand left for Upfluence after nine months, citing being unable to import an existing list and limited export. The vendor replied in public that CSV, Google Sheets, Zapier and API imports all exist and that the account manager should have explained them — which is a support failure rather than a missing feature, but it cost that customer nine months.
  • • You cannot sign yourself up. A 2024 reviewer never received the verification code and found no contact route; the vendor's July 2026 reply confirms sign-up is now guided onboarding only, through support or a demo request.

Where the ratings disagree. The distribution matters more than the average here. 58 of the 62 reviews are five stars, and everything that costs money — the payout fee, the import and export ceiling, the dead self-serve sign-up, the size of team the platform assumes — sits in the three one-star reviews and the single three-star one. Read the 4.8 and you learn nothing you can budget against; the four low reviews take about two minutes.

Three months up front is the price, not one

Take the headline figures and turn them into what you will be charged, because this vendor quotes three different numbers for the same plan. Standard is $649 a month billed monthly, $519 billed quarterly, $454 billed annually. Pro is $1,049, $839 or $734. Business is $2,490, $1,992 or $1,743. The public pricing page leads with the middle column, so the price you have probably seen is already a 20% discount, and the annual column takes 30% off the same monthly rate.

Both check out to the cent. With the three-month minimum on every plan and no free trial, the entry ticket is a quarter: $1,947 on monthly billing, $1,557 on quarterly. On annual you are committing $5,448 to Standard, $8,808 to Pro or $20,916 to Business. Now the ladder. Per reach-out at the quarterly rate, Standard is about 52 cents, Pro about 17 and Business about 20. The cheapest new relationship on the page is Pro's, not Business's — Business buys UGC tracking, custom API integrations, more payouts and eight seats, and it costs more per reach-out to get them.

On annual the same pattern holds at 45, 15 and 17 cents. That makes the tier question unusually clean, and it is not really about volume. Because follow-ups are free and only new contacts draw the quota, most brands will not exhaust 1,000 new creators a month. What moves people up is seats — one, three, eight — and payouts, at 20, 200 and 500. A two-person team paying creators is on Pro from day one regardless of how many influencers it contacts.

One genuine saving that does not appear in the tier list: payouts run through bank debit at 0.1% to 0.4%, where a card processor would typically take 2-3%. On $20,000 of creator payments a month that is a difference of roughly $320 to $580 — real money against the subscription, and worth putting into the comparison.

How to actually use Influencer Hero

Decide the billing term before the tier, because it changes the price by a quarter. Standard is $649 a month billed monthly, $519 quarterly and $454 annually, and the public pricing page shows you the quarterly figure by default. Whatever you are quoted, ask which column it comes from. Then size yourself on the right unit, which is not the one most people assume. A reach-out is counted once, when you first contact a new influencer; every follow-up, reply and continuing conversation with that creator is unlimited and never touches the quota again.

So the question is not how many messages you will send but how many new creators you will open a relationship with in a month. For most brands that number is well under Standard's 1,000, which means volume is rarely what decides the tier. What usually decides it is seats and payouts. One seat, 20 payouts on Standard; three seats and 200 on Pro; eight and 500 on Business. If two people will work in the tool, or you pay more than twenty creators a month, you are on Pro whatever your outreach looks like.

Plan for the cap before you meet it. Exceed your reach-outs and you can buy a top-up or move up a tier; the vendor's own documentation advises the upgrade, on the grounds that brands who top up once tend to keep doing it until the saving is gone. No top-up rate is published, so ask for it in writing while you are still negotiating rather than when you are already over.

Then set up payouts on the cheap rail. Business carries 500 influencer payouts a month and only Enterprise is unlimited (pricing page re-read 24 September 2026). Fund payouts by bank direct debit rather than card: the vendor puts the fee at 0.1% to 0.4% and its Payout Manager post at around 0.4% to 0.5% effective, with US ACH capped at $5 a payout, which is the quietest saving in the product once you pay creators at any volume.
