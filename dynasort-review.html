What buyers report about Dynasort

Hands-on: tested 2026-11-17Reported by buyers: Shopify App Store listing apps, read September 2026

Themes from the buyer-review sites listed at the foot of this page, read in September 2026. We tested Dynasort ourselves on 2026-11-17, but one test is a narrow sample, so here is what other buyers report. We summarise it here because the complaints are usually the part a vendor page leaves out.

What they praise
  • • Tenure is the unusual part of this set. Reviewers write at almost three years, at over a year twice, and at eleven months of continuous use: a different signal from a wall of first-week enthusiasm. The long-tenure reviewers are retailers in Israel, the United States and the United Kingdom.
  • • Sorting on margin is described as a money outcome rather than a convenience. An Australian bathware retailer at about a year says they make better margins because they can prioritise the products that earn well, in an industry where sell prices track the market.
  • • A US golf retailer says the app's ordering converts at a higher rate than Shopify's own. A UK fabric merchant gives the concrete version: out-of-stock products pushed to the bottom of every collection, hours saved each week against doing it by hand, and recipes flexible enough to follow the seasons as new stock replaces last year's sellers.
  • • It is the app that survived a bake-off at high SKU counts. A US fashion brand lists Algolia, Boost, Sort'd and Fast Simon as tools they had used and says Dynasort is the only one that could handle what they do.
  • • One support name recurs across independent reviews, Wade, described as answering within minutes, building custom work including multi-location views, and fixing a year's single issue on the spot.
What they complain about
  • • The single negative review is worth reading in full before you install. A US merchant three days in calls it a completely non-working app and a waste of time, and says two days of support produced generic replies with no specifics and that the basic rules could not be set up.
  • • The vendor's public reply to that review names a cause that belongs on any buyer's checklist: the merchant's theme was overriding Shopify's manual sort order by defaulting to best-selling, a theme-level setting that bypasses whatever a collection app writes through the Shopify API. Check your theme's default collection sort before you judge the app: by the vendor's account it affects every app in this category, not only this one.
  • • The scoring takes learning. A UK merchant nine days in says it takes a while to understand the logic of the scoring before it becomes powerful, which is the same opacity our own note on this page flags.
  • • The base is small and English-speaking: fifteen reviews from the United States, the United Kingdom, Israel and Australia, and not one of them describes a failure mode other than the theme conflict above.

Where the ratings disagree. Fifteen reviews is a thin base and the shape is all-or-nothing: fourteen fives and one single star, with nothing in between. What makes it more useful than a larger pile of first-week praise is the spread over time: the set runs from November 2024 to June 2026, and several reviewers are writing at one to three years of continuous use, which is the durability evidence a new app cannot offer. It also helps that the one bad experience has a documented technical cause the vendor answered in public, so you can check whether it applies to your theme rather than guessing.

    How to actually use Dynasort

    Use the free Demo tier properly rather than as a preview: it sorts 2 collections around the clock, which is exactly enough for a controlled comparison. Pick your best-selling collection and one you suspect is badly ordered, record the conversion rate and revenue per visit for both before you change anything, and let a single recipe run for a full two weeks. Start with one clear signal (push sold-out products down, or sort by sell-through) before you blend margin and inventory into it, because a recipe built from six signals at once is one you cannot reason about when a product disappears from view.

    Watch which products drop as much as which ones rise; that is where automated merchandising goes wrong. Then check the serve allowance against your traffic (10,000 a month on Demo, 100,000 on Basic) and take the full 30-day trial to see a real sales cycle.
